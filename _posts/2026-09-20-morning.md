### Budget: How the government 'sees double' when it comes to savings


The budget is the planning of a country's expenses and revenues for a certain period, typically a year. Governments seek to reduce public spending by encouraging savings, which is known as austerity fiscal policy. However, there are several ways for the government to "double count" savings:

1. Hidden taxes: some taxes are hidden in consumption taxes or administrative fees. For example, a value-added tax (VAT) may be higher than necessary to finance public spending.
2. Subsidies: governments can offer subsidies to businesses to encourage their growth and job creation, but these subsidies can also be used to finance public spending.
3. Borrowing: governments can borrow money to finance their spending, but they can also use the interest paid on these loans as a source of revenue.
4. Privatization: when the government sells public companies to individuals or private companies, it can receive an immediate sum of money, but it also loses a regular income from the sold company. However, governments can use this money to reduce their debt and improve their budget.
5. Unreported expenses: some governments may hide expenses by not declaring them in the official budget. For example, they may use public funds to finance political projects or election campaigns.
6. Off-budget accounts: some governments have created off-budget accounts to manage public spending without including them in the official budget. For example, they may use these accounts to finance development projects or social programs.
7. Inter-agency transfers: some governments may transfer funds between different public agencies without declaring them in the official budget. For example, they may use these transfers to finance expenses that are unpopular or to avoid reducing important programs.
8. Secret accounts: some governments have created secret accounts to manage public funds without declaring them to the public or parliamentarians. For example, they may use these accounts to finance defense projects or intelligence programs.

In conclusion, there are several ways for the government to "double count" savings, but these methods can be controversial and lack transparency for the public.
